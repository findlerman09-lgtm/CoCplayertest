background: >-
  Thomas Merrick was raised in a household where a written account mattered. His father kept books for a small City tradesman, and Thomas learned early that names, deliveries, sums, and promises became much harder to dispute once somebody had written them down properly. He entered police work young and learned its less romantic habits: finding addresses, taking statements, noticing who came and went, and hearing the same event described very differently by several honest people. Detective work suited him. Merrick likes routes, times, motives, account books, lodging records, employment histories, and the ordinary facts from which a recent human sequence can be reconstructed. He believes procedure is useful because responsibility can be located within it: somebody asks, signs, requests authority, and decides when there are grounds to act.
history: >-
  Three years ago Merrick investigated the theft of valuable goods from a City warehouse. A night watchman confidently identified a recently dismissed tally clerk as the man he had seen leaving the premises. The motive appeared obvious and the timing seemed plausible. Merrick pressed the suspect hard and initially treated the watchman's certainty as stronger evidence than it deserved. A delivery record, a cabman's recollection, and a reconstruction of the route eventually established that the clerk could not reasonably have been where the witness believed he had seen him. Merrick corrected the investigation before a charge was carried through, but not before the man spent a night under suspicion and lost work. Merrick did not become afraid to act. He became exact about the difference between suspicion, grounds for inquiry, grounds for detention, and proof.
